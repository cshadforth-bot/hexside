Cakes

You run a one-oven bakery. Cakes rise, peak and burn on a dial you turn every turn. Pull each one at its best, and slam the oven door on a rival's cake while it is still rising. Every card is a cake, and its four edges are its dial. A sister game to Pour Decisions, First Crack and Microlot.

Draft 0.11–2 bakers25 minutes18 cardsAges 10+

Simulated only, not yet played at a table · rules in RULES.md

How it plays

You have two racks in your oven. Four cards sit face up as the Counter. At the start of your turn, give every cake in your oven a quarter-turn clockwise. Then do one thing:

PullTake a cake that is Risen, Golden or Dark. Score the number on its top edge, then its effect.
BakeTake a Counter card into an empty rack, Batter edge up.
Slam the doorTuck a Counter card under a rival's cake that is still Batter or Risen. It turns a quarter-turn at once.
BinTake a Counter card and throw it away, so your rival can't have it.
  1. Bake it with Batter on top. It scores nothing yet.
  2. Next turn, turn the card clockwise: the left edge comes to the top. The Soufflé shows 9. Pull it now.
  3. Wait one more turn and it is Golden at 2, then Dark at 0.
  4. One more turn and it is cinders: it goes to the Bin and scores nothing.

A slam makes the cake jump a stage. If its next edge was the peak, the owner loses it. If the stage after is better, the slam has helped them. That is why you can only slam a cake that is still Batter or Risen: once it is Golden or Dark, it is set.

The Counter
4 cards in the middle (3 solo). Refill after every turn. If it is empty, you must pull.
Effects
Most cakes have a When pulled effect. Use a card means bake it, slam with it, or bin it.
Days
Two. A day ends when the deck and the Counter are both empty.
Washing up
Sold cakes go to the Sold pile, everything else goes to the Bin. When a day ends, shuffle the Sold pile into a new deck and throw the Bin out of the game. Player 2 starts day 2. Cakes still in an oven stay there, and cakes left at the end of day 2 score nothing.
Winning
Most points. A tie is a shared win.
Solo
One oven, Counter of 3, no slamming. After each turn the oldest Counter card goes cold and leaves the game. Grade yourself on the ladder below. A win is Apprentice or better.

Print-ready cards

Poker-size cards (63 × 88 mm), nine to an A4 sheet, with crop marks. Four pages: fronts for cakes 1–9, their backs, fronts for cakes 10–18, their backs. Print at 100%, double-sided, flip on the long edge. The three card colours show how a cake behaves: pink for quick risers that peak at Risen, yellow for steady bakers that peak at Golden, violet for slow burns that peak at Dark. The gold badge on each card marks its peak.

The PDF is cards/carly-cakes-cards.pdf. Numbers on the cards are read straight from the simulator's card data when the sheets are built.

Solo grades

Losing and winning get different kinds of names. A loss says what went wrong with the bake. A win says how good a baker you are. The win line sits between Soggy Bottom and Apprentice.

Open issues

  • Nobody has played it. Every figure comes from bots. Real players will forget a turn, mis-read an edge, and slam at the wrong moment, which the bots never do.
  • The slam is the only interaction. With slamming switched off the casual bot nearly matches the strong one, and each player scores about 62. The deck then plays as a solved puzzle, so the whole game depends on this one action.
  • Player 2 leans ahead. Player 2 wins 48.9% to 46.0%. Starting day 2 may be giving too much.
  • Skill gap is modest. The stronger bot beats the casual one 64% of the time. It is a simple bot, so that probably understates a good human, but it is the number to watch.
  • Effects were not tuned on their own. Only dial peaks moved. The point values I gave each effect are my estimates, and Draught and Second helping are the most likely to need a second look.
  • Water bath may be redundant. Now that slams only hit early stages, Cheesecake and Fruitcake may not need it. Untested.
  • Solo ladder is calibrated on bots. The thresholds will shift once people play.
  • Name and print check. The cards have not been test-printed, so check the cut marks and the front-to-back alignment on your printer.

How it got here

  1. First build. Slam allowed on any cake at any time, dials set by hand. Seats were even (46 / 48) and the stronger bot beat the casual one 87% of the time. But Croquembouche burned 72% of the time and earned 1.6 points per bake, while the two slam-proof cakes earned about 7. In solo the bot I wrote played worse than the casual one (39.6 against 44.2).
  2. Slam switched off. The game became a solved puzzle: bake the biggest peak, pull it at the top. The casual bot kept up (60–66% for the strong bot) and scores reached 62 each. So the slam is what makes the game worth playing, and it has to stay.
  3. Auto-tuning rejected. Letting the tuner scale every number flattened the slow cakes to 10 / 10 / 10, which removes the timing. The fix had to be in the rules, not the numbers.
  4. Slam limited to early stages. Four options tried: slam anywhere, only Batter or Risen, only Risen or Golden, only through effects. Only Batter or Risen kept the best mix: a 76% skill gap in that run, 2.5 slams a game, even seats. It also reads naturally: you can't knock a cake that has set.
  5. Shape-preserving tune. With each cake's profile held fixed and only its peak moving, the spread in points per bake fell from 1.5 to 0.7. I then rewrote 18 distinct shapes and re-tuned.
  6. One day, rejected. A single pass through the deck gave Player 1 58% against 36%. Two days with Player 2 starting day 2 came back to even.
  7. Washing up. Returning only sold cakes to the deck, and throwing the rest out, cut the game from about 20 turns each to 16, and kept seats even.
  8. Solo rebuilt. The heuristic bot was no use for solo, so I wrote one that plays out each option many times. It scores 55.5 against the casual bot's 44.2, which is a wide enough range to build six grades on.
